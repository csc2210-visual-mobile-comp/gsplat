\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)~\cite{kerbl2023} has rapidly become the dominant
paradigm for novel view synthesis, offering high visual quality with a real-time rendering 
and fast training. By representing scenes as collections of explicit, differentiable 
3D Gaussian primitives rendered via fast tile-based rasterization, 
3DGS achieves high visual fidelity with training times measured in minutes. 
This has sparked a large body of follow-on work spanning
dynamic scenes~\cite{lr4dgstream}, scene editing~\cite{gaussianeditor}, compression~\cite{Papantonakis_2024, survey3dgs}, 
and avatar generation~\cite{avatar}.

Despite this success, training 3DGS on memory-constrained hardware remains
difficult~\cite{kerbl2023, Papantonakis_2024, survey3dgs}. Peak GPU memory during 
training is driven by two coupled factors.
First, 3DGS employs an adaptive densification strategy that continuously clones
and splits Gaussian primitives throughout optimization, growing the primitive count
$N$ from an initial sparse point cloud to several million --- each new Gaussian
adding to every active parameter tensor. Second, each Gaussian carries a substantial
per-primitive parameter budget. Beyond position, opacity, and covariance, the
view-dependent color of each primitive is encoded by spherical harmonic (SH)
coefficients. Using degree-3 SH, each Gaussian stores $(3+1)^2 = 16$ coefficients
per RGB channel, totaling 48 floating-point values per Gaussian. As noted in prior
memory analyses~\cite{Papantonakis_2024, survey3dgs}, these SH coefficients alone account
for more than half of all model parameters --- for a scene with $N = 3$ million
Gaussians, this amounts to roughly 576\,MB before optimizer states and gradients
are accounted for. The combination of a growing $N$ and a large per-Gaussian
footprint makes peak training VRAM a hard constraint that existing methods have
largely failed to address.

A natural response is to reduce $N$ directly. Pruning-based methods such as
Mini-Splatting~\cite{minisplatting} and LP-3DGS~\cite{lp3dgs} remove redundant
Gaussians to produce more compact representations. However, aggressive pruning often
trades reconstruction quality for memory, since each Gaussian encodes fine
geometric and appearance detail that cannot always be recovered once removed.
We take a different position: rather than reducing the number of primitives,
we ask whether the \emph{per-Gaussian parameter cost} can be reduced adaptively
--- allocating more capacity to primitives that genuinely require it and less
to those that do not --- without compromising the Gaussian count or the
reconstruction quality it affords.

This question has been explored in prior work, but only after training completes.
Reduced3DGS~\cite{Papantonakis_2024} introduces an adaptive per-Gaussian SH band
selection based on view-dependent transmittance variance, substantially reducing
storage and rendering memory. Critically, the authors acknowledge that this
approach does not reduce \emph{peak training memory}, since the full SH tensor
must be maintained throughout optimization before any pruning can occur.
LFGS~\cite{lfgs} similarly applies dynamic SH masking post-training for
compression. SG-Splatting~\cite{sgsplatting} and MEGS2~\cite{megs2} replace
SH with spherical Gaussian lobes for more compact rendering, but again leave
the training regime unchanged. The gap is clear: no existing method reduces
per-Gaussian parameter cost \emph{during} training, where peak VRAM is
actually determined.

Our approach is to apply Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)~\cite{lora} directly to
the learnable parameters of each Gaussian primitive during training. LoRA,
originally proposed for parameter-efficient fine-tuning of large language models,
replaces a parameter matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with a low-rank
factorization $W \approx AB$, where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$,
$B \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$, and $r \ll \min(m, n)$, reducing parameter
count from $mn$ to $r(m + n)$. By maintaining only the low-rank factors
throughout training, the full parameter matrix is never allocated, directly
reducing peak VRAM rather than compressing after the fact.

Among the Gaussian attributes, the SH coefficient tensor is the natural primary
candidate for low-rank factorization. Its structure as a matrix
$C \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 16}$ (RGB channels $\times$ SH basis functions)
makes the decomposition well-defined, and its dominance in the per-Gaussian
memory budget makes it the highest-leverage target. The observation that
Reduced3DGS~\cite{Papantonakis_2024} can prune high-degree SH bands from a large
fraction of Gaussians with minimal quality loss supports our hypothesis that
the effective rank of $C$ is low for most primitives. Furthermore, 
rather than applying a fixed rank across all primitives, we draw inspiration from adaptive 
budget allocation in language models~\cite{adalora} to dynamically distribute rank. 
A flat matte surface requires far less representational capacity than a complex specular 
reflection; our method dynamically models this heterogeneity.
We also explore LoRA applied to other Gaussian attributes to characterize where low-rank structure
is and is not beneficial; these experiments and their outcomes are reported in
Section~\ref{sec:method}.

LoRA has seen use in the 3DGS ecosystem, but not for this purpose.
LR-4DGStream~\cite{lr4dgstream} applies low-rank adaptation to a deformation
model for dynamic scene streaming bandwidth reduction. UFV-Splatter~\cite{ufvsplatter}
injects LoRA into a feed-forward Gaussian-prediction network to handle unfavorable
camera poses. In both cases, LoRA is applied to auxiliary neural network weights,
not to Gaussian primitive parameters directly. StyleSplat~\cite{stylesplat} comes
closest in spirit, directly optimizing the SH coefficients of selected Gaussians
via a feature-matching loss to transfer artistic style --- but without any
low-rank constraint or memory reduction goal. To our knowledge, no prior work
applies low-rank factorization to the parameters of Gaussian primitives during
training as a mechanism for reducing peak GPU memory.

\section{Goal}
\label{sec:goal}

he primary goal of this project is to drastically reduce peak GPU training memory 
in 3DGS, enabling high-fidelity scene optimization on memory-constrained, consumer-grade 
hardware. We achieve this by replacing full-rank Gaussian parameters with per-Gaussian 
low-rank factorizations. By dynamically allocating rank across primitives based on 
their estimated view-dependent complexity, we aim to eliminate standard memory 
bottlenecks without reducing the overall Gaussian count or sacrificing rendering quality.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

%% -------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Low-Rank Parameterization}

For a scene with $N$ Gaussian primitives, consider a target parameter block
(e.g., the higher-order spherical harmonic coefficients) whose per-Gaussian
contribution has dimension $D$. Instead of optimizing an unconstrained
$[N \times D]$ parameter matrix, we decompose the \emph{adjustment} to a
frozen base as:
\[
  \Delta = A B, \quad A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times r},\; B \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times D},
\]
where $r \ll D$ is the rank. $A$ is a per-Gaussian matrix (one row per
primitive), and $B$ is a \emph{shared} projection matrix across all Gaussians.
The effective parameter used during rasterization is then:
\[
  \theta_{\text{eff}} = \theta_{\text{base}} + \Delta,
\]
where $\theta_{\text{base}}$ is frozen after initialization. $A$ is optimized
per-Gaussian while $B$ is optimized globally with a separate optimizer.

%% -------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Static LoRA on Target Selection}
\label{sec:static_lora}

Our first instantiation applies the low-rank parameterization with a fixed
rank $r$ to a chosen subset of Gaussian attributes throughout training.
The zero-order SH coefficient (band 0, encoding the mean color) is always
left as a directly optimized parameter.

\subsubsection{Flexible Target Selection}

The parameterization supports any subset of Gaussian attributes as LoRA
targets, enabling controlled ablations over which attributes benefit from a
low-rank constraint. As a concrete example, consider jointly targeting colors
(degree-3 SH, $D_c = 48$), scales ($D_s = 3$), and rotations ($D_q = 4$),
giving a combined dimension $D = 55$. The shared matrix
$B \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times 55}$ is partitioned column-wise as
$B = [B_c \mid B_s \mid B_q]$. For Gaussian $i$ with per-Gaussian row
$a_i \in \mathbb{R}^{r}$, the effective attributes are:
\begin{align}
  \theta^{(i)}_{\text{color}} &= \theta^{(i)}_{\text{color, base}} + a_i B_c, \\
  \theta^{(i)}_{\text{scale}} &= \theta^{(i)}_{\text{scale, base}} + a_i B_s, \\
  \theta^{(i)}_{\text{quat}}  &= \theta^{(i)}_{\text{quat, base}}  + a_i B_q.
\end{align}
The same low-dimensional latent vector $a_i$ jointly modulates all targeted
attributes through the respective column blocks of $B$. The frozen base tensors
have gradients disabled, so no gradient buffers or Adam optimizer states are
allocated for them.

\subsubsection{LoRA-Aware Densification}

Standard densification (clone, split, prune) must be extended to account for
the LoRA parameterization: when a Gaussian is cloned or split, the corresponding
rows of $A$ must be duplicated; when Gaussians are pruned, those rows must be
removed. We use a LoRA-aware densification strategy that wraps the default
densification logic with this bookkeeping.

\subsubsection{Effective Parameter Computation}

At each training step, $\theta_{\text{eff}}$ is computed and passed directly
to the rasterizer. Regularization losses also operate on the effective
parameters when the corresponding attribute is LoRA-targeted, ensuring
penalization is applied to the quantities that actually influence rendering.

%% -------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Settling on Higher-Order SH}

Through ablation we find that applying LoRA to geometry attributes (positions,
scales, rotations) degrades reconstruction quality, as these attributes require
precise, high-rank updates to represent sharp geometry. We therefore restrict
LoRA to the higher-order SH coefficients (bands 1--3), targeting only the
view-dependent color component. This is also the highest-leverage target from
a memory standpoint, as the higher-order SH tensor dominates the per-Gaussian
parameter budget.

\subsection{Dynamic Rank Allocation via Gradient-Based Binning}
\label{sec:dynamic_lora}

The static approach assigns the same rank $r$ to every Gaussian regardless of
its view-dependent complexity. We extend it with a dynamic rank mechanism that
periodically reallocates rank across primitives based on observed gradient
activity.

\subsubsection{Rank Bins}

Rather than tracking a continuous rank per Gaussian, we maintain three discrete
rank bins: $r_{\min}$, $r_{\text{mid}} = 8$, and $r_{\max}$. All Gaussians
are initialized in the $r_{\min}$ bin. Each bin owns a compact parameter tensor
$A^{(r)} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_r \times r}$, where $N_r$ is the number of Gaussians
currently assigned to rank $r$, together with its own Adam optimizer. Because
the parameter and optimizer state tensors have dimension proportional to $N_r
\cdot r$ rather than $N \cdot r_{\max}$, memory scales with \emph{actual} rank
usage rather than the worst case.

The shared matrix $B \in \mathbb{R}^{r_{\max} \times D}$ is defined at the
maximum rank. For a Gaussian in bin $r$, only the first $r$ rows of $B$ are
used:
\[
  \Delta^{(i)} = a^{(i)} \, B_{:r,\,:\,}, \quad a^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^{r}.
\]

\subsubsection{Gradient Accumulation}

To measure how much representational capacity each Gaussian demands, we
accumulate a per-Gaussian gradient magnitude after every backward pass.
For Gaussian $i$ in bin $r$, the accumulated gradient is the sum of absolute
gradients of its $A$ row:
\[
  g^{(i)} \mathrel{+}= \left\| \nabla_{a^{(i)}} \mathcal{L} \right\|_1.
\]
At the end of each allocation interval (one full pass over the training set),
the accumulated gradient is normalized by the current rank to make it
comparable across bins:
\[
  \tilde{g}^{(i)} = \frac{g^{(i)}}{r^{(i)}}.
\]

\subsubsection{Rank Reallocation}

Reallocation is triggered once per epoch (one full pass over the training set)
and is disabled after densification stops, allowing the rank distribution to
stabilize for the remainder of training.

\paragraph{Partitioning.}
Given a quota $(q_{\text{top}},\, q_{\text{mid}},\, q_{\text{bot}})$ summing
to 1, we compute quantile thresholds on the
normalized accumulated gradient $\tilde{g}$ across all Gaussians and assign
each primitive to a bin:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the top $q_{\text{top}}$ fraction (highest $\tilde{g}$) are assigned
    rank $r_{\max}$,
  \item the bottom $q_{\text{bot}}$ fraction (lowest $\tilde{g}$) are assigned
    rank $r_{\min}$,
  \item the remaining middle fraction are assigned $r_{\text{mid}}$.
\end{itemize}
The accumulated gradient buffer is then zeroed so the next interval measures
fresh gradient activity.

\paragraph{Rebuilding bin tensors.}
After the new rank assignments are computed, the per-bin parameter tensors and
their Adam optimizers are rebuilt. For each Gaussian, let $r_{\text{old}}$ and
$r_{\text{new}}$ denote its rank before and after reallocation, and let
$k = \min(r_{\text{old}}, r_{\text{new}})$ be the number of columns that
remain active.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Retained columns} ($1, \ldots, k$): parameter values and Adam
    first and second moment estimates are copied over from the old bin tensor.
  \item \emph{New columns} ($k{+}1, \ldots, r_{\text{new}}$, upranked only):
    parameter values are initialized with small random noise; Adam moments
    are initialized to zero.
  \item \emph{Dropped columns} ($k{+}1, \ldots, r_{\text{old}}$, downranked
    only): parameter values and optimizer state are discarded.
\end{itemize}
Each new bin gets a fresh Adam optimizer whose step counter is reset to zero,
so bias correction restarts while the carried-over momentum remains effective
for retained columns.

\subsubsection{Rank Heatmap}

To visualize the learned rank distribution, we render a per-Gaussian heatmap
by replacing each Gaussian's color with a blue-to-red colormap encoding its
current rank (blue = $r_{\min}$, red = $r_{\max}$). This provides an
interpretable view of which scene regions require high representational
capacity for view-dependent appearance.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Target Selection}

We evaluate the effect of LoRA target selection on reconstruction quality
across three scenes (Garden Spheres, Sedan, Toy Car), measuring PSNR (dB,
higher is better). The full-rank SH3 baseline is trained without any LoRA
modification.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{PSNR (dB) for different LoRA target configurations. \textbf{Bold}
indicates the best result among color-targeting configurations per scene.
Geometry-only rows are included for reference and are not candidates for
memory reduction in our setting.}
\label{tab:target_selection}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Target & Garden Spheres & Sedan & Toy Car \\
\midrule
Full-rank SH3 (baseline) & 23.29 & 25.16 & 25.01 \\
\midrule
Colors only              & 23.08 & \textbf{25.14} & 24.85 \\
Colors + quats           & \textbf{23.10} & 25.02 & \textbf{24.87} \\
Colors + opacities       & 22.95 & 25.05 & 24.78 \\
Colors + scales          & 22.94 & 24.40 & 24.81 \\
Colors + quats + scales  & 22.94 & 24.44 & 24.81 \\
\midrule
Quats + scales (ref.)           & 23.14 & 24.30 & 24.98 \\
Quats + scales + opacities (ref.) & 23.12 & 24.35 & 25.05 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The results show that all LoRA configurations remain competitive with the
full-rank baseline, with quality gaps generally under 0.5 dB. Restricting LoRA
to colors only achieves the closest match to the baseline on the Sedan scene
(25.14 vs.\ 25.16 dB) and performs consistently well across all three scenes.
Adding rotations to the color target (colors + quats) produces nearly identical
results to colors only, suggesting rotations carry low-rank structure that
LoRA can capture without loss.

The most notable degradation comes from including scales in the target set:
configurations with scales drop by approximately 0.75 dB on Sedan compared to
colors only, indicating that scale parameters benefit from full-rank updates to
faithfully represent fine geometric detail. Interestingly, targeting only
geometry attributes (quats + scales, with or without opacities) achieves
competitive PSNR on Garden Spheres and Toy Car but underperforms on Sedan,
suggesting scene-dependent sensitivity to geometry LoRA.

Overall, colors only provides the most consistent and predictable behavior
across scenes, making it the preferred target for the dynamic rank extension.
