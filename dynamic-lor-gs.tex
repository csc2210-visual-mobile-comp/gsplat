\begin{abstract}
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) achieves state-of-the-art novel view synthesis but 
suffers from prohibitive memory consumption, largely driven by the high-degree 
Spherical Harmonic (SH) coefficients required for view-dependent color. Current 
memory-reduction techniques, such as reducing SH degrees or applying uniform 
low-rank approximations (LoRA), apply uniform constraints to all primitives. 
This restriction degrades high-frequency specular details while wasting 
representational capacity on flat, diffuse geometry. In this paper, we present 
a Dynamic LoRA architecture that heterogeneously allocates parameter capacity 
across the scene based on physical complexity. By tracking representational 
demand via accumulated gradients, our system routes Gaussians into discrete 
rank bins using percentile-based quotas, scaling natively alongside geometric 
densification. This enables the network to automatically constrain capacity for 
matte surfaces while prioritizing complex, specular regions. Our evaluation 
shows that dynamic routing achieves perceptual parity (LPIPS) with 
full-capacity SH3 baselines at a fraction of the VRAM footprint. Furthermore, 
it consistently outperforms uniform Static LoRA models operating under the same 
effective parameter budget, demonstrating the advantage of scene-aware 
parameter distribution for 3DGS compression.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
The introduction of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)~\cite{kerbl2023} has significantly 
advanced novel view synthesis by providing an explicit, point-based scene 
representation that achieves real-time rendering at high resolutions. However, 
this fidelity incurs a substantial computational cost. The explicit nature of 
3DGS requires maintaining millions of independent primitives, resulting in a 
large GPU memory (VRAM) footprint during both training and inference. A 
majority of this parameter budget is consumed by the higher-order Spherical 
Harmonic (SH) coefficients, which are necessary to model complex, 
view-dependent color phenomena such as reflections and specular highlights. 

To mitigate this bottleneck, recent works propose various compression 
strategies, ranging from geometric pruning~\cite{minisplatting, lp3dgs} to 
reducing the maximum allowed SH degree across the scene~\cite{Papantonakis_2024, 
lfgs, sgsplatting, megs2}. Alternatively, applying standard Low-Rank 
Adaptation (LoRA)~\cite{lora} to the SH parameters can compress the footprint. 
However, these static approaches share a fundamental limitation: they assume 
the scene's view-dependent complexity is uniform. In physical reality, scenes 
are highly heterogeneous. A matte wall requires minimal view-dependent 
capacity, whereas a glossy car window requires precise, high-frequency 
parameterization. Imposing a uniform parameter constraint forces the 
over-parameterization of diffuse surfaces while limiting the capacity available 
to render complex specularities accurately.

In this work, we address the limitations of uniform parameter allocation. We 
propose a Dynamic LoRA architecture that profiles and distributes 
representational capacity based on the localized demands of the scene. By 
factorizing the higher-order SH coefficients into a shared projection matrix 
and per-Gaussian low-rank latents, we create a flexible parameter space. During 
the geometric densification phase, we track the gradient activity of each 
primitive to quantify its demand for high-frequency details. Using a 
percentile-based routing mechanism to avoid spatial starvation, our system 
dynamically migrates Gaussians between discrete rank bins. This ensures that 
memory is allocated where the physical topology of the scene demands it.

In summary, our main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We introduce a Dynamic LoRA architecture for 3DGS that 
    factorizes higher-order SH coefficients to enable heterogeneous, 
    per-primitive capacity allocation.
    \item We develop a routing mechanism based on normalized gradient activity 
    and percentile quotas, allocating higher representational capacity to 
    complex primitives while preventing spatial starvation.
    \item We demonstrate empirically that our dynamic allocation achieves 
    perceptual parity with full SH3 baselines at significantly reduced VRAM, 
    and consistently outperforms uniform static baselines operating under 
    identical effective parameter budgets. 
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)~\cite{kerbl2023} is a leading framework for novel 
view synthesis. By representing scenes with explicit, differentiable 3D 
Gaussian primitives rendered via fast tile-based rasterization, 3DGS achieves 
high-fidelity real-time rendering and fast training. This efficiency has 
motivated extensive follow-on work in dynamic scenes~\cite{lr4dgstream}, 
editing~\cite{gaussianeditor}, 
compression~\cite{Papantonakis_2024, survey3dgs}, and avatar 
generation~\cite{avatar}.

Despite this success, optimizing 3DGS on memory-constrained hardware remains 
challenging due to two coupled factors. First, adaptive densification 
continuously clones and splits primitives, expanding the population $N$ to 
several million. Second, each Gaussian requires a substantial parameter budget. 
Because view-dependent color is modeled by degree-3 spherical harmonic (SH3) 
coefficients, each primitive stores 48 floating-point values just for color. 
These coefficients dominate the memory footprint, consuming roughly 576\,MB for 
a 3-million Gaussian scene before accounting for optimizer states and 
gradients~\cite{Papantonakis_2024, survey3dgs}. Consequently, peak training 
VRAM is a critical bottleneck that existing methods struggle to fully resolve.

Current memory-reduction strategies generally fall into two categories: 
pruning and post-training compression. Pruning methods such as 
Mini-Splatting~\cite{minisplatting} and LP-3DGS~\cite{lp3dgs} reduce $N$ 
directly, which can degrade fine geometric detail. Alternatively, compression 
techniques like Reduced3DGS~\cite{Papantonakis_2024}, LFGS~\cite{lfgs}, 
SG-Splatting~\cite{sgsplatting}, and MEGS2~\cite{megs2} reduce the per-Gaussian 
footprint through SH band masking or lobe replacement. Because these operations 
occur \emph{after} training completes, the full SH tensor must be maintained 
throughout optimization, leaving peak training memory unaffected.

We propose reducing the per-Gaussian parameter cost adaptively \emph{during} 
training using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)~\cite{lora}. LoRA replaces a 
parameter matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with a low-rank factorization 
$W \approx AB$ (where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$, 
$B \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$, and $r \ll \min(m, n)$). Maintaining only 
these low-rank factors directly reduces peak VRAM allocation.

The SH tensor $C \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 16}$ is the primary target for this 
factorization. Because methods like Reduced3DGS~\cite{Papantonakis_2024} 
successfully prune high-degree bands with minimal quality loss, we hypothesize 
the effective rank of $C$ is inherently low for most primitives. Inspired by 
adaptive budget allocation in language models~\cite{adalora}, we dynamically 
distribute this rank rather than applying a uniform constraint. This approach 
leverages the fact that matte surfaces require less representational capacity 
than complex specularities.

While LoRA has been introduced to the 3DGS ecosystem, it is typically applied 
to auxiliary neural network weights for dynamic streaming~\cite{lr4dgstream} or 
pose prediction~\cite{ufvsplatter}. StyleSplat~\cite{stylesplat} optimizes SH 
coefficients directly for style transfer but lacks a low-rank constraint. To 
our knowledge, this is the first work to apply low-rank factorization directly 
to 3DGS primitive parameters during optimization to reduce peak training memory.

\section{Motivations and Objectives}
\label{sec:motivations}
While the peak training VRAM bottleneck of 
3DGS~\cite{kerbl2023, Papantonakis_2024, survey3dgs} is well-documented, 
current mitigation strategies rely on uniform constraints that fail to account 
for the physical heterogeneity of real-world environments. Our primary 
motivation is grounded in the observation that view-dependent complexity 
varies significantly across a scene. A diffuse surface, such as a painted wall, 
exhibits minimal color variation across viewing angles and warrants only a few 
coefficients. Conversely, highly specular objects, such as a glossy car 
chassis, require precise, high-frequency parameterization spanning the full SH 
band. Assigning a uniform parameter budget to every Gaussian primitive is 
therefore structurally inefficient. Furthermore, while applying a static LoRA 
uniformly across the scene reduces total memory, it limits the capacity 
available for high-frequency details while over-parameterizing simple geometry,
forcing an unnecessary compromise between VRAM savings and perceptual fidelity.

Driven by these observations, our core objective is to decouple a scene's 
geometric resolution from its view-dependent memory footprint. We aim to 
design a dynamic, memory-aware routing architecture that autonomously 
distributes parameter capacity based on localized topological needs in the form 
of quantized low-rank buckets. By continuously evaluating the demand of each 
primitive and actively readjusting its LoRA rank \emph{during} the 
densification phase, this project seeks to demonstrate that adaptively 
allocating memory strictly to the regions that mathematically require it can 
significantly reduce peak training VRAM while preserving the high-frequency 
perceptual quality of the rendered scene.

\section{Ideas and Methodologies}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Isolating the Memory Bottleneck}
For a scene with $N$ Gaussian primitives, consider a target parameter block
(e.g., the higher-order spherical harmonic coefficients) whose per-Gaussian
contribution has dimension $D$. Instead of optimizing an unconstrained $[N 
\times D]$ parameter matrix, we decompose the \emph{adjustment} to a frozen 
base using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)~\cite{lora} as:
\begin{align}
  \Delta = A B, \quad A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times r},\; B \in 
  \mathbb{R}^{r \times D},
\end{align}
  
where $r \ll D$ is the rank. $A$ is a per-Gaussian matrix, and $B$ is a 
\emph{shared} global projection matrix. The effective parameter used during 
rasterization is:
\begin{align}
  \theta_{\text{eff}} = \theta_{\text{base}} + \Delta,
\end{align}
where $\theta_{\text{base}}$ is frozen after initialization. $A$ is optimized
per-Gaussian, while $B$ is optimized globally via a separate optimizer.

Alternatively, the full parameter matrix can be reconstructed directly as:
\begin{align}
  M = A B, \quad \theta_{\text{eff}} = A B,
\end{align}
provided the magnitude of $\theta_{\text{eff}}$ is sufficiently small.

Our initial implementation applies this parameterization with a fixed rank $r$ 
to selected Gaussian attributes. The zero-order SH coefficient (mean color) 
remains a directly optimized parameter to preserve base color fidelity.

\subsubsection{Flexible Target Selection}
This architecture supports any subset of Gaussian attributes as LoRA targets. 
For example, jointly targeting colors (SH3, $D_c = 48$), scales ($D_s = 3$), 
and rotations ($D_q = 4$) yields a combined dimension $D = 55$. The shared 
matrix $B \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times 55}$ is partitioned column-wise as 
$B = [B_c \mid B_s \mid B_q]$. For Gaussian $i$ with latent vector 
$a_i \in \mathbb{R}^{r}$, the effective attributes are:
\begin{align}
  \theta^{(i)}_{\text{color}} &= \theta^{(i)}_{\text{color, base}} + a_i B_c, \\
  \theta^{(i)}_{\text{scale}} &= \theta^{(i)}_{\text{scale, base}} + a_i B_s, \\
  \theta^{(i)}_{\text{quat}}  &= \theta^{(i)}_{\text{quat, base}}  + a_i B_q.
\end{align}
A single latent vector $a_i$ modulates all targeted attributes. We implement 
these updates as residuals to accommodate rotation and scale adjustments. 
Because frozen base tensors lack gradients, no Adam optimizer states are 
allocated for them.

\subsubsection{LoRA-Aware Densification and Computation}
Standard densification must be wrapped with LoRA-aware bookkeeping: cloning or 
splitting a Gaussian duplicates its corresponding row in $A$, while pruning 
removes it. During training, $\theta_{\text{eff}}$ is computed dynamically and 
passed to the rasterizer, ensuring regularization losses correctly penalize the 
effective parameters influencing the render.

\subsubsection{Settling on Higher-Order SH}
Ablation studies revealed that applying LoRA to geometry (positions, scales, 
rotations) degrades reconstruction quality, as these require precise, high-rank 
updates to represent sharp structural boundaries. Therefore, we restrict LoRA 
to higher-order SH bands (1--3). Because this tensor dominates the per-Gaussian 
parameter budget, it is simultaneously the most effective target for memory 
reduction.

\subsection{Dynamic Resource Allocation}
We iteratively designed and evaluated several dynamic allocation strategies to 
distribute capacity based on estimated primitive complexity.

\subsubsection{Global Masking as a Proof of Concept}
To validate the rasterizer's ability to handle heterogeneous capacities, we 
first implemented a global masking strategy. We allocated a single high-rank 
tensor for the entire scene and simulated dynamic allocation by applying a 
binary mask during the forward pass to silence higher-order dimensions for 
selected Gaussians. While the approach is VRAM-inefficient due to the 
underlying dense allocation, this confirmed that heterogeneous rank 
representations maintain training stability, justifying the development of a 
genuinely sparse architecture.

\subsubsection{Limitations of Absolute Scalar Thresholds}
Our initial active migration strategy employed absolute scalar thresholds to 
promote or demote ranks based on gradient magnitudes. Empirical testing 
demonstrated this approach is brittle. Baseline gradient magnitudes fluctuate 
significantly across datasets depending on material properties (e.g., matte vs. 
reflective). Attempts to use statistical bounds, such as means or median 
multipliers, also failed. Looser bounds resulted in static allocations, while 
tighter bounds induced frequent rank thrashing as gradients oscillated during 
local error minimization. This establishes that absolute thresholding is 
ill-suited for the dynamic topology of 3DGS.

\subsubsection{Gradient-Based Scoring and Percentile Routing}
To quantify representational demand, we track gradient activity. For Gaussian 
$i$ assigned to rank $r^{(i)}$, we accumulate the absolute gradients of its 
latent vector $a^{(i)}$ after each backward pass:
\begin{equation}
  g^{(i)} \mathrel{+}= \left\| \nabla_{a^{(i)}} \mathcal{L} \right\|_1
\end{equation}
Post-epoch, this value is normalized by the current rank 
($\tilde{g}^{(i)} = g^{(i)} / r^{(i)}$) to enable cross-bin comparison, and 
the buffer is reset.

To map these scores ($\tilde{g}$) to VRAM constraints, we compared fixed 
numerical quotas against percentile distributions. Theoretically, fixed quotas 
offer the minimal memory footprint: by establishing absolute bounds, the system 
can statically pre-allocate tensors, completely bypassing the VRAM overhead 
associated with dynamically slicing, copying, and migrating tensor entries. 
However, empirical implementation revealed a baseline ``materialization 
cost''---a structural memory lower bound that both fixed and percentile routing 
inevitably encounter. Because this shared floor negates the theoretical memory 
advantage of static pre-allocation, we use percentile quotas 
$(q_{\text{top}}, q_{\text{mid}}, q_{\text{bot}})$. Although this requires 
dynamic tensor migration, percentiles scale natively with the evolving 
geometry, guaranteeing a consistent parameter-to-topology ratio.

\subsubsection{Scene Analysis for Relative Thresholding}
Because optimal quota fractions are scene-dependent, we require a mechanism to 
estimate them early in training, prior to reaching peak GPU memory. Inspired by 
post-training variance analysis~\cite{Papantonakis_2024}, we evaluated two 
predictive signals during the densification phase: SH energy 
($\|A_i B_i\|_F^2$) and initial color variance across training views:
\begin{equation}
  s_i^{\text{color}} = \frac{1}{3}\left(
    \mathrm{Var}(R_i) + \mathrm{Var}(G_i) + \mathrm{Var}(B_i)
  \right)
\end{equation}
We evaluate the predictive accuracy of these signals against a step-15,000 
full-SH3 gold standard, using GMM clustering to determine boundaries 
automatically.

\subsubsection{Experimental Setup}
All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 Ti (12 GB 
VRAM) running Ubuntu 24.04.4 LTS with Python 3.12.13, PyTorch 2.11.0, CUDA 
12.8, and gsplat 1.5.3. We evaluate on three scenes from the 
Ref-NeRF~\cite{refnerf} real dataset (\textit{gardenspheres}, \textit{sedan}, 
and \textit{toycar}), downsampled by a factor of 4. Five model variants are 
compared: LoRA Complete, LoRA Fixed, LoRA Static, SH3, and SH2. All variants 
are trained for 15,000 steps with Gaussian densification stopped at step 
10,000, a batch size of 1, and an SSIM loss weight of 0.2. To account for 
variance in Gaussian initialization and densification, each variant is run 
three times per scene, yielding 45 total training runs. Models are evaluated at 
steps 7,000 and 15,000 using PSNR, SSIM~\cite{ssim}, and LPIPS~\cite{lpips} 
(AlexNet).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
To evaluate the computational and perceptual trade-offs of our architecture, we 
assess models across four primary metrics: Peak training VRAM (GB), PSNR, SSIM, 
and LPIPS (AlexNet). Because PSNR inherently favors over-smoothed pixel 
averages, we prioritize LPIPS as the primary indicator of preserved 
high-frequency, view-dependent specularities. Within our evaluation context, 
meaningful algorithmic improvements are defined as VRAM reductions of 
$\ge0.5$\,GB, PSNR deltas of $\ge0.1$\,dB, and LPIPS reductions of $\ge0.01$.

\subsection{Overall Performance and Memory Reduction}
Table~\ref{tab:overall_results} presents the quantitative evaluation of our 
Dynamic LoRA architecture against the full-capacity Gold Standard (SH3) and a 
uniform Static LoRA baseline. Across all datasets, our dynamic routing achieves 
an optimal quality-to-memory tradeoff, significantly reducing the peak training 
VRAM footprint while preserving perceptual fidelity.

Compared to the SH3 baseline, our method achieves substantial memory savings: 
reducing peak VRAM by $\sim26\%$ on the heavily populated 
\textit{Garden Sphere} scene ($7.08 \rightarrow 5.18$\,GB) and up to $36\%$ on 
the \textit{Sedan} scene ($2.90 \rightarrow 1.85$\,GB). Notably, despite the 
bookkeeping overhead of maintaining dynamic routing indices, our method 
achieves a memory footprint that is either highly competitive with or strictly 
superior to the uniform Static LoRA baseline (e.g., achieving the lowest 
footprint on the \textit{Sedan} dataset). 

Importantly, this VRAM reduction does not incur a proportional penalty to 
rendering quality. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:overall_results}, Dynamic LoRA 
consistently secures the ``best'' or ``second-best'' position across most 
quantitative metrics. In terms of PSNR and SSIM, our architecture closely 
matches the unconstrained SH3 baseline, demonstrating that dynamically 
constraining parameters does not destabilize geometric convergence. 
Furthermore, on the highly complex \textit{Garden Sphere} dataset, our dynamic 
method outperforms the SH3 Gold Standard in LPIPS ($0.217$ vs. $0.219$). This 
validates our core architectural hypothesis: by adaptively allocating parameter 
capacity strictly to regions that demand it, the network is forced to learn 
robust structural representations rather than overfitting high-degree Spherical 
Harmonics to noise, thereby maintaining sharp, view-dependent realism at a 
fraction of the hardware cost.

\subsection{Attribute Ablation for LoRA Targeting}
We evaluate the effect of LoRA target selection on reconstruction quality 
(PSNR) across three scenes (\textit{Garden Sphere}, \textit{Sedan}, 
\textit{Toycar}). The full-rank SH3 baseline operates without any LoRA 
constraint.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:target_selection}, all LoRA configurations remain 
competitive with the full-rank baseline, maintaining quality gaps generally 
under $0.5$\,dB. Isolating LoRA strictly to colors achieves the closest match 
to the baseline on the \textit{Sedan} scene ($25.14$ vs. $25.16$\,dB) and 
performs consistently across all scenes. Expanding the target to include 
rotations yields nearly identical results, suggesting rotations possess an 
inherent low-rank structure that LoRA captures without significant loss.

Conversely, including scales in the target set introduces the most notable 
degradation. Configurations targeting scales drop by approximately $0.75$\,dB 
on \textit{Sedan} compared to the colors-only approach, indicating that scale 
parameters require full-rank updates to preserve fine geometric detail. 
Furthermore, targeting solely geometry attributes (rotations and scales) 
achieves competitive PSNR on \textit{Garden Sphere} and \textit{Toycar} but 
underperforms on \textit{Sedan}, highlighting a scene-dependent sensitivity 
to geometric factorization.

Overall, targeting only colors provides the most predictable and robust 
behavior across varying topologies, justifying its selection as the exclusive 
target for our dynamic rank architecture.

\subsection{Evaluating Heuristics for Scene-Aware Partitioning}
Across all combinations of scoring signal and clustering method, rendering 
quality differences were negligible (PSNR range 24.33--24.44\,dB, standard 
deviation $\sim$1.0\,dB across three scenes). This suggests that the choice of 
per-Gaussian scoring signal is less critical than the overall quota 
distribution.

Figure~\ref{fig:scene_dep} shows gold-standard quota fractions across seven 
scenes using color variance. These fractions vary substantially with the 
low-rank bucket ranging from 0.42 to 0.59, confirming that scene-independent 
fixed quotas are ineffective and justifying scene-specific estimation.

Figure~\ref{fig:predictor_quality} compares prediction error over training 
steps for color variance and SH energy, evaluated under rank-2 and rank-32 
warmups. Because color variance depends solely on Gaussian geometry, it is 
unaffected by warmup rank and consistently outperforms SH energy. Conversely, 
SH energy begins with large errors because the LoRA factors lack meaningful 
initialization. While the rank-32 model recovers by step 2100, the rank-2 
model remains volatile until step 6000, and both remain less accurate than 
color variance. We therefore train at rank~2 until step 4000 and run a single 
color variance pass to establish scene-specific quotas. This strategy minimizes 
memory consumption through the densification phase while providing a reliable 
quota estimate before reaching peak memory.

\subsection{Effective Rank Analysis and Architectural Trade-offs}
To isolate the specific impact of our dynamic routing mechanism, we evaluate 
our architecture against a uniformly allocated Static LoRA baseline and a 
standard reduced-capacity SH2 baseline. To ensure a fair comparison, all models 
operate under a roughly equivalent parameter budget: SH2 natively utilizes 
$15$ parameters per channel, Static LoRA is fixed uniformly at $r=16$, and 
our Dynamic LoRA averages an effective rank of $r \approx 16.4$ across the 
population. 

By architectural definition, standard SH2 establishes the lower bound for 
memory consumption (e.g., $4.50$\,GB on the \textit{Garden Sphere} scene). 
Because LoRA-based methods require maintaining localized pointer arrays, 
bucket indices, and segregated Adam optimizer states, they inherently carry a 
VRAM overhead that prevents them from outperforming SH2 in pure memory 
reduction at the same effective rank. 

However, evaluating these representations strictly through global quantitative 
metrics (PSNR and SSIM) obscures the critical advantage of heterogeneous 
allocation. While all methods achieve effective parity in these global averages, 
spatial averaging dilutes localized perceptual improvements. Standard SH2 and 
Static LoRA constrain every primitive to a uniform capacity, limiting the 
reconstruction of complex, high-frequency specularities. Consequently, while 
they save memory, they tend to over-smooth fine details. 

In contrast, our dynamic architecture leverages the same average parameter 
budget but distributes it heterogeneously. As demonstrated by the LPIPS 
metric, which heavily penalizes the loss of sharp, view-dependent textures, 
dynamic routing consistently outperforms both SH2 and Static LoRA on complex 
scenes. By constraining flat, diffuse geometry that requires minimal capacity, 
the system frees parameters to prioritize complex specular regions. This 
demonstrates that dynamic rank distribution is structurally superior to a 
static uniform rank: it matches the physical topology of the scene, achieving 
higher perceptual realism without requiring a larger global parameter budget.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{Active Allocation vs. Post-Hoc Pruning}
Mallick et al.~\cite{taming3dgs} address peak training VRAM by replacing 
unconstrained densification with a budget-constrained constructive process. 
While this systematically degrades quality as Gaussian counts ($N$) shrink, it 
primarily targets geometric redundancy. Our work is fundamentally orthogonal, 
targeting representation redundancy within the SH coefficients. Because high 
spatial frequency does not strictly correlate with high view-dependency, these 
approaches are complementary. As Papantonakis et al.~\cite{Papantonakis_2024} 
establish that primitive count and SH storage are the two dominant memory 
bottlenecks in 3DGS, a hybrid approach is highly promising. Combining 
budget-constrained densification with our dynamic LoRA rank assignment would 
enable a dual-budget optimization framework. Such a system could allocate 
resources precisely by spending the ``Gaussian budget'' on geometric detail 
and the ``Rank budget'' on complex view-dependent effects. This approach 
would likely achieve a more favorable Pareto frontier than either method in 
isolation.

\subsection{Material-Driven Limits and the Oracle Problem}
A critical limitation arises in globally uniform environments (e.g., the matte 
\textit{Toycar} dataset), where perfectly aligned native representations (e.g., 
universal SH1) outperform our dynamic architecture by avoiding tensor slicing 
and routing overhead. This exposes an oracle problem in memory-efficient 
3DGS: mathematically optimizing the percentile quotas ($q_{\text{top}}, 
q_{\text{bot}}$) requires prior knowledge of the scene's final material 
complexity. However, because geometry is volatile during early densification, 
this complexity is only revealed \emph{by} training. This creates a functional 
impasse: perfectly configuring the memory-saving architecture requires a full 
training run, which inherently renders the architecture itself redundant. 
Resolving this paradox necessitates future work into pre-optimization 
estimators capable of predicting scene complexity directly from the initial 
sparse point cloud.

\subsection{Implementation Overhead and Future Integration}
Despite requiring per-bucket optimizers and periodic reallocation, our 
implementation achieves memory savings close to the theoretical limit. For the 
\textit{Garden Sphere} scene, our average rank of 16.4 predicts a theoretical 
saving of 1.48 GB against a measured 1.31 GB. This 0.17 GB gap (similarly 
minimal in \textit{Sedan} and \textit{Toycar}) is achieved through strict 
Python-level memory management, explicitly clearing stale optimizer states 
during rank reallocation. The remaining discrepancy represents a 
framework-level materialization cost. Specifically, temporary dense tensors 
must be allocated during densification and forward passes to interface 
fragmented bucket matrices with standard framework logic. To completely 
eliminate this indexing overhead and maximize hardware efficiency, 
variable-rank memory pointers must eventually be integrated into custom 
C++/CUDA kernels. Nevertheless, our implementation validates the architectural 
viability of dynamic capacity routing.

\section{Conclusion}
We demonstrated that the prohibitive memory footprint of 3DGS is primarily a 
consequence of uniform parameter allocation. Our Dynamic LoRA architecture 
employs gradient-based percentile routing to identify and exploit 
heterogeneous representational demands, automatically constraining diffuse 
geometry to prioritize capacity for complex specularities. Consequently, our 
method achieves the perceptual realism of a full-capacity SH3 model at a 
fraction of the memory cost. While current framework-level tensor management 
imposes a minor materialization overhead, the architectural viability of 
dynamic routing motivates the next critical step: integrating variable-rank 
parameter resolution directly into custom CUDA kernels. By aligning mathematical 
capacity with physical scene topology, we provide a scalable foundation for 
memory-efficient 3DGS architectures.
